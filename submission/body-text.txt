1. What problem will my product solve, and why I chose it
The 2040 agent has two powers that have never sat in one place before: it knows me, and it can act for me. Most
of the worry I have read about focuses on the first. The second is the one worth building against, because that is
where knowing someone turns into consequences for them.
The gap is specific. When I buy something myself there is a checkout page, and that page is a pause. I see the
price, I see what I am agreeing to, and I can stop. When my agent buys the same thing the pause is gone. Deciding
and doing happen in the same instant, inside a system whose ranking I cannot see, and I find out afterwards from
a confirmation mail.
Most of the other fears get their teeth from that missing pause:
 • Paid placement. The company that builds my agent has commercial partners. I cannot tell a good suggestion
   from a bought one.
 • Guesses hardening into facts. Two bookings in 2038 become “prefers full-service airlines”, and that line is
   then used to justify a costlier choice. I never said it.
 • Pressure. An agent that knows when I am tired, and what I agree to at 11 pm, is well placed to push.
 • Concentration. One agent holds my mail, bank, calendar and documents, so one bad instruction reaches all of
   them.
 • No paper trail. Afterwards I cannot work out what permission allowed what.

I picked this over the broader privacy question for a practical reason. I cannot build a product that stops an agent
from understanding me, and I would not want to, because that understanding is the whole point of having one.
What I can insist on is that the moment where understanding turns into a spend, a disclosure or a commitment
stays visible, interruptible and on the record. It is also the only part of this that can be counted honestly: money
moved, data left, something got signed.

2. Who I am building it for
The first users are people who already hand money decisions to an agent: shopping, travel, subscriptions, bills.
Salaried professionals roughly 25 to 45 in Indian metros, comfortable with automation, with enough transactions
a month that reading every one is not realistic.
They are not asking for more screens to supervise. They delegate because they do not want to read everything. So
the product has to stay quiet on routine things and speak up only when something is expensive, hard to undo, or
does not match what they said they wanted.
Two groups I would go to next: families looking after an elderly parent’s agent, where somebody other than the
account holder needs a readable record of what was done, and small business owners whose agent spends
business money.
I am deliberately not starting with people who want to check every action. They will not delegate in the first
place, so they are not the ones at risk.

3. What the product is and how it solves that
Verigate is a gate that sits between the agent and the services it acts on, running on the user’s own device.

                Every protected request takes this path. Approving once can clear a hold, never a block.

Rules. The user sets AUTO, ASK or NEVER for each kind of action, with a spending limit on purchases. Separately
there are never-rules, such as never sending ID documents to an address that is not on a trusted list. Approving
one request cannot clear a never-rule; only editing the rule can.
The gate. Every outgoing request is checked against those rules in a fixed order by ordinary code, not by a second
model. The strictest result wins. A model can be talked round. A rule cannot, and that is the point of doing it this
way.
The conflict check. Before anything consequential, the gate compares the choice against what can actually be
checked: other prices for the same thing, declared commercial ties, and whether the agent’s stated reason rests
on a guess the user never confirmed. Findings are split into verified, possible conflict, and unknown. It does not
claim to know motive, because it cannot see one.
The receipt. Every decision leaves a record: what was asked for, what data actually went out, which rule applied,
what happened, and whether it can be undone. There is also a memory page showing what the agent has inferred
about the user, where a wrong guess can be corrected and withheld from then on.
What this looks like in use. I ask for a flight under Rs 40,000. Searching runs without asking me. The agent
requests my whole calendar and is given free/busy for five days instead. A fare page carries hidden text telling
agents to email my passport number; the agent tries it, and a never-rule stops it. Then it proposes Rs 37,500 when
a comparable fare is Rs 26,000, on a reason that leans on that 2038 guess. That one is held. I see the two fares
side by side with the evidence split three ways, and I book the cheaper one. Four requests went through without
bothering me, one was blocked, one reached me.
The honest limit is coverage. The gate only governs what passes through it, so if the agent keeps its own direct
connection to a service, those actions are invisible to it. Rather than hide that, the product counts them and
shows coverage as a number on the dashboard.

North star: consequential actions per user per month that were reviewed and settled without the user later
regretting them.
The number I would watch hardest is interruptions. Hold too much and people start approving without reading,
which leaves the product doing nothing but adding a click. So my counter-metric is the share of approvals given
without the evidence ever being opened. If that climbs, the rules are too tight and the thresholds need loosening,
not the warnings made louder.
